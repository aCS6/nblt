B Grammar

Linking Ideas

1 Ways of linking ideas

  • Conjunctions (e.g. but, because, although) join two clauses in one sentence.
  • Adverbial expressions (e.g. consequently, however, furthermore) connect ideas across separate sentences — they go at the beginning, middle or end of a sentence.
  • Prepositions (e.g. in spite of, because of, despite) come before a noun phrase or -ing form.

2 Linking expressions

PurposeConjunctionsAdverbialsPrepositions
Adding informationand, as well asalso, besides, furthermore, in addition, likewise, moreover, similarly—
Sequencing—first, next, after that, then; firstly, secondly, finally—
Cause/reasonbecausetherefore, consequently, so, thus, as a resultbecause of, due to, on account of, owing to
Resultsotherefore, consequently, as a result—
Contrastingbut, although, thoughhowever, nevertheless, on the other hand, yet, in contrast, alternatively, on the contraryin spite of, despite
Giving examples—for example, that is to say, in other words, i.e.—
Concluding—to sum up, in conclusion, overall—
⚠ because of / due to / owing to + noun phrase (NOT a full clause).
Use because before a clause: because the weather was bad.
Use because of before a noun: because of the bad weather.
Use due to the fact that or owing to the fact that before a clause.
⚠ in spite of / despite + noun/-ing form (NOT a full clause).
Use despite the fact that + clause: Despite the fact that it was raining…
Sequencing: Use firstly/secondly/finally to order points in an argument.
Use first/next/after that/then to order activities in a process.

Grammar extra: Written vs spoken linking expressions

More common in writing

thus, therefore, finally, furthermore, hence, moreover, nevertheless, in addition, firstly, secondly, finally

More common in speech

so, then, anyway

Experience versus speed

Certain mental functions slow down with age, but the brain compensates in ways that can keep seniors as sharp as youngsters.

Jake, aged 16, has a terrific relationship with his grandmother Rita, who is 70. They live close by and they even take a Spanish class together twice a week. On one occasion Rita tells Jake, 'I think it's great how fast you pick up new grammar. It takes me a lot longer.' Jake replies, 'Yeah, but you don't seem to make as many silly mistakes on the quizzes as I do. How do you do that?'

In that moment, Rita and Jake stumbled across an interesting set of differences between older and younger minds. Popular psychology says that as people age their brains 'slow down'. However, although certain brain functions such as perception and reaction time do indeed take longer, that slowing down does not necessarily undermine mental sharpness. Indeed, evidence shows that older people are just as mentally fit as younger people because their brains compensate for some kinds of declines in creative ways that young minds do not exploit.

Just as people's bodies age at different rates, so do their minds. As adults advance in age, the perception of sights, sounds and smells takes a bit longer, and laying down new information into memory becomes more difficult. The ability to retrieve memories also quickly slides and it is sometimes harder to concentrate and maintain attention.

On the other hand, the ageing brain can create significant benefits by tapping into its extensive hoard of accumulated knowledge and experience. The biggest trick that older brains employ is to use both hemispheres simultaneously to handle tasks for which younger brains rely predominantly on one side. Electronic images taken by cognitive scientists at the University of Michigan have demonstrated that even when doing basic recognition or memorization exercises, seniors exploit the left and right side of the brain more extensively than men and women who are decades younger. Drawing on both sides of the brain gives them a tactical edge, even if the speed of each hemisphere's process is slower.

In another experiment, Michael Falkenstein of the University of Dortmund found that when elders were presented with new computer exercises they paused longer before reacting and took longer to complete the tasks, yet they made 50% fewer errors, probably because of their more deliberate pace.

Neurologists at Northwest University came to this conclusion after analyzing 50 people ranging from age 23 to 78. The eldest participants did just as well on the tests as the youngest did, and yet the MRI scans indicated that in the elders' brains, the areas responsible for language recognition were much less active. Darren Gleitman, who headed the study, concluded that older brains solved the problems just as effectively but by different means.
