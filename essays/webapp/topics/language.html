Language

Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

As computers translate quickly and accurately, learning foreign languages is a waste of time. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Some people believe that because translation technology has become fast and accurate, learning foreign languages is no longer necessary. I disagree with this view, as I believe language learning still offers benefits that technology cannot replace.

It is true that translation apps have improved significantly and can now handle everyday communication reasonably well. Travellers can use their phones to translate menus, signs, or simple conversations, which makes short trips abroad more manageable without formal language study. For people who only need occasional translation for basic tasks, relying on technology may seem like a practical solution.

However, learning a language provides advantages that go far beyond simple translation. Speaking another language allows people to understand cultural nuances, humour, and expressions that technology often translates literally and inaccurately. Building genuine relationships, whether socially or professionally, is much easier when people can communicate directly rather than through a screen. For example, business negotiations often rely on subtle tone and trust, which can be lost when conversations pass through translation software. In addition, learning a new language has cognitive benefits, such as improving memory and problem-solving skills, which have nothing to do with translation ability at all.

In conclusion, although translation technology is undoubtedly useful for basic communication, I disagree that it makes learning foreign languages a waste of time. The cultural understanding, personal connections, and mental benefits gained through language learning remain valuable regardless of technological progress.

Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

Scientists predict that all people will choose to talk the same global language in the future. Do you think this is a positive or negative development?

Some scientists predict that the world will eventually adopt a single global language. In my opinion, this would be a largely negative development, despite some practical benefits it might bring.

One clear advantage of a single global language is improved communication between people from different countries. Business, travel, and international cooperation would become much easier if everyone could communicate without language barriers. Misunderstandings caused by translation errors would also become far less common, which could improve efficiency in fields such as diplomacy and international trade.

Despite these benefits, I believe the negative consequences would outweigh the advantages. Language is closely tied to culture and identity, and losing linguistic diversity could mean losing valuable aspects of different cultures, including traditional stories, humour, and ways of thinking that do not always translate directly into another language. If everyone spoke only one language, many smaller languages could eventually disappear, along with unique cultural knowledge passed down through generations. For example, indigenous languages already at risk of extinction could vanish entirely if a single global language became dominant, resulting in permanent cultural loss.

In conclusion, while a single global language could make communication more convenient, I believe this development would be largely negative due to the resulting loss of cultural diversity and identity. Protecting linguistic diversity is, in my opinion, more valuable than the convenience a shared language would provide.

Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

Some schools are no longer teaching children how to write with a pen. Do you think children should learn handwriting skills?

In some schools, children are no longer taught how to write by hand, as typing has become the more common method of writing. I believe children should still learn handwriting skills, even though digital writing has become increasingly important.

One reason handwriting remains valuable is its connection to learning and memory. Research suggests that writing by hand helps children remember information better than typing, since the physical process of forming letters engages the brain differently. For young children in particular, learning to write by hand also supports the development of fine motor skills, which are important for other everyday tasks as well.

In addition, handwriting remains a practical skill in daily life. Despite the popularity of computers and smartphones, people still need to write by hand occasionally, such as filling out forms, taking quick notes, or signing documents. Without proper handwriting practice, children may struggle with these simple tasks later in life. Furthermore, being unable to read or write cursive script could make it difficult for children to read historical documents or personal letters written by older generations.

However, I do recognise that typing skills are equally essential in today's digital world, since most work, from school assignments to professional jobs, now takes place on computers. Schools should therefore aim to teach both skills rather than replacing one with the other entirely.

In conclusion, I believe handwriting should still be taught in schools due to its cognitive and practical benefits, even though typing skills are equally necessary. A balanced curriculum that includes both would best prepare children for both traditional and modern demands.

Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

Some people think that a person can never understand the culture of a country unless they speak the language. Do you agree with this opinion?

Some people believe that understanding a country's culture is impossible without speaking its language. I partly agree with this view, as language provides deep cultural insight, although I believe some cultural understanding is still possible without full fluency.

One reason I support this opinion is that language often carries meanings and expressions that cannot be fully understood through translation alone. Idioms, humour, and traditional sayings frequently reflect a country's values, history, and way of thinking, and these nuances are often lost when converted into another language. For example, understanding certain jokes or proverbs in a foreign language usually requires more than a literal translation; it requires knowledge of the cultural context behind them. This means fluent speakers often gain a much deeper appreciation of a country's traditions and social norms than those who rely solely on translation.

However, I do not believe language is the only path to cultural understanding. People can still learn a great deal about a country's culture through direct experience, such as travelling, observing daily life, participating in local customs, or building relationships with local people, even with limited language ability. Many travellers develop a genuine appreciation for a country's traditions and values simply by immersing themselves in everyday life there, even without becoming fluent speakers.

In conclusion, while I agree that speaking a language provides valuable insight into a country's culture, I do not believe fluency is absolutely necessary for meaningful cultural understanding. Direct experience and openness to a culture can also contribute significantly, even without complete language mastery.

Opinion (Agree/Disagree)

Some people think that you can never become fluent in a language unless you have spent time living or working in that country. To what extent do you agree?

Some people argue that fluency in a foreign language is impossible without living or working in the country where it is spoken. I only partly agree with this view, as I believe fluency can be achieved through other methods, although immersion certainly makes the process easier.

It is true that living in a country provides constant exposure to the language, which greatly accelerates learning. Being surrounded by native speakers forces learners to practise speaking and listening daily, often in real situations such as shopping, working, or socialising. This kind of practical experience helps learners understand natural pronunciation, everyday vocabulary, and cultural context in ways that classroom learning alone often cannot provide. For example, someone who works in a foreign country is likely to develop more natural conversational skills than someone who only studies from textbooks.

However, fluency is not impossible without living abroad. With modern technology, learners can now access native speakers, immersive content, and interactive language exchange platforms without leaving their home country. Many people have become fluent through dedicated self-study, regular practice with online tutors, and consistent exposure to films, podcasts, or books in the target language. Motivation and consistent effort often play a larger role in achieving fluency than location alone.

In conclusion, while living or working abroad certainly helps learners achieve fluency more naturally, I disagree that it is absolutely necessary. With sufficient dedication and the right resources, fluency can still be achieved without leaving one's home country.

Advantages & Disadvantages

Some people think that all children in school should learn a foreign language at an early age. What are the advantages and disadvantages of doing this?

Some people believe that children should begin learning a foreign language from a young age in school. This approach offers several benefits, although it also presents certain challenges worth considering.

One major advantage of early language learning is that young children generally acquire new languages more easily than adults. Their brains are particularly receptive to picking up pronunciation and grammar naturally during childhood, often resulting in more native-like fluency later in life. In addition, learning a second language from an early age can improve cognitive skills such as memory, problem-solving, and multitasking, since switching between languages exercises the brain in unique ways. For example, children who grow up bilingual often perform well in tasks requiring flexible thinking.

On the other hand, introducing a foreign language too early can present certain difficulties. Some young children may struggle to balance learning a new language alongside mastering their first language, particularly if they are still developing basic literacy skills. This could potentially cause confusion or slow progress in both languages during the early stages of learning. Additionally, effective language teaching requires well-trained teachers and appropriate resources, which not all schools, especially those with limited funding, may be able to provide consistently.

In conclusion, while early language learning offers valuable cognitive and linguistic benefits, it also requires careful implementation to avoid confusing young learners and to ensure quality teaching is available. When properly supported, however, I believe the advantages of learning a foreign language early outweigh the potential drawbacks.
